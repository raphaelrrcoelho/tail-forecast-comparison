Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Comparing tail forecasts: the
number of exceedances is the sample size", for publication in the Journal of
Forecasting.

Forecasters routinely rank competing tail forecasts by their scores: 99%
prediction intervals for load and prices, extreme quantiles in epidemic
forecasting and, under the market-risk rules, 97.5% value-at-risk and
expected shortfall. The standard tool is the Diebold-Mariano test. The paper
shows that such comparisons rest on the number of exceedances rather than
the number of observations, and draws consequences that readers of the
Journal of Forecasting can apply directly:

1. How much data a comparison needs. A lower bound that holds for every
   test, and a confidence sequence that attains it with no distributional
   assumptions, valid when the comparison is monitored over time.
2. When the Diebold-Mariano test can be trusted. With few exceedances it
   favours the narrower or less conservative forecaster, whichever is
   better; the usual small-sample corrections (Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold,
   fixed-smoothing) do not fix this, while a bootstrap-t version largely
   does. The paper gives a rule of thumb for the number of exceedances
   below which normal critical values should not be used.
3. Expected shortfall. A less conservative ES forecast can be shown to be
   worse without assumptions, but never shown to be better without an
   assumption on the loss tail.

Two applications illustrate the results: online conformal prediction
intervals on seven forecasting data sets, and (value-at-risk, expected
shortfall) forecasts for seven stock indices. In 250-day backtesting
windows, significant Diebold-Mariano results that favour the less
conservative model contradict the ranking on the remaining days in 18.4% of
cases, against 6.9% for those that favour the more conservative model.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. Code that reproduces
every result is provided as supporting information.
